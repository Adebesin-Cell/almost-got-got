01

First, the habits

Beats almost everything

You don't need to know every trick. A handful of habits stops most of them before they start.

01

Slow down. Urgency is the weapon.

Every scam needs you to act now, before you think, before you check. The rush is the tell. Give it ten minutes and most of them fall apart.

02

If you're not sure, ask someone.

A moment of hesitation can save you everything. Ask a friend, ask the bank, ask anyone. No real request ever suffers from being checked.

03

Don't tap links you didn't expect.

Not the "your account is locked" text, not the prize, not the delivery notice. Go to the site yourself. A link is the cheapest thing a scammer owns.

04

Your codes and passwords are yours alone.

OTP, PIN, password, nobody real ever needs them. Not the bank, not "support," not a friend in a hurry. The moment someone asks, that's the scam.

05

Verify on a channel you already trust.

Call back on the number you saved, not the one they gave you. Reach them on an app you already use. Never let them choose how you check.

06

A face or a voice is no longer proof.

AI can fake a voice note, even a video call. Trust the check, not the familiarity. If it sounds like someone you know asking for money, verify anyway.

i

Impersonation leads

Most reports began the same way: someone pretending to be a bank, an official, or a person the victim already knew. Trust is the door every one of them walks through.

ii

Not one was a "hack"

No virus. No breached system. Every single report was a person being persuaded to act. The vulnerability was human judgement under pressure, which is exactly why policy, not software, is the defence.

iii

Warning is what worked

Of those who avoided the scam, most had been told beforehand what it would look like, a bank officer's warning, a story from a friend. Knowing the move is the counter to the move.

iv

The ask is always money or a code

Transfers, OTPs, marketplace payments, "processing fees." However the story starts, it funnels to one thing: moving value out of your reach and into theirs.

v

It's happening here

Akure, Lagos, Ibadan, Abuja, Kano, Kaduna, Port Harcourt, Uyo, Benin, Yola, Bauchi, Sapele, and more, twenty-one places named in all, with one report from the United States. Not "somewhere on the internet", in our own towns, to people we know, this week.

04

The case file

Same moves, bigger targets

A fair objection to any survey like ours: these are students and ordinary people, so maybe the professionals are fine. They are not. Below are documented incidents, each one publicly reported, each one a person with real security training making the same call our respondents made. The move does not change when the target gets sophisticated. Only the size of the loss does.

i

The developer who clicked the link

September 2025: a maintainer of chalk and debug, npm packages pulled billions of times a week, received an email from support@npmjs.help saying his two-factor credentials needed updating or the account would be locked. He updated them on the attacker's page. Eighteen packages were poisoned within the hour, and the payload reached every project that installed them. He is a career developer. The email was an OTP-reset scare, play No. 01, with a lookalike domain, play No. 04. Reported by The Hacker News, Wiz and Trend Micro.

ii

A security company's own staff, nine days ago

22 August 2026: attackers registered a lookalike domain, stood up a fake single-sign-on page, then phoned ReliaQuest employees one by one, each caller naming a real colleague from the security team. One teammate entered their password and approved the push notification. ReliaQuest sells threat detection for a living. What saved them was not that nobody fell for it, someone did, it was device trust blocking the attacker's laptop from every application behind that login. ReliaQuest's own published incident report, 25 August 2026.

iii

Sixteen days before that, and again this month

Levi Strauss disclosed on 10 August 2026 that social engineering got attackers onto three employees' work computers. On 21 August, Apollo Global Management, a firm managing over a trillion dollars, confirmed attackers had talked their way into its cloud systems and reached Social Security numbers. Neither was a software exploit. Both were conversations. Reported by The Register and Bloomberg.

iv

When the code came from the phone in his pocket

August 2023: Retool staff got a text claiming IT needed them to fix an account issue affecting their healthcare coverage, timed to a real Okta migration already underway. One employee signed in on the fake portal. The attacker then cloned that employee's voice and called the IT desk for one more code. Because Google Authenticator had recently begun syncing codes to the cloud, breaking into the Google account handed over every other code too. Twenty-seven customer accounts followed. Retool's own post-incident write-up; BleepingComputer, September 2023.

v

The meeting where everyone was fake

January 2024: a finance employee at Arup's Hong Kong office was suspicious of an email from the CFO asking for a confidential transaction, so he did the responsible thing and joined a video call to confirm it. The CFO was there. So were colleagues he recognised. Every face and voice on that call was generated from Arup's own public videos. He made fifteen transfers totalling roughly $25 million. Verification is only verification if the channel is one you chose. Reported by CNN, May 2024.

vi

And it is accelerating

Thirty-seven separate npm supply-chain campaigns hit 497 packages in the first half of 2026 alone, against fourteen campaigns and 111 packages across all of 2025. On 4 August 2026 the maintainer of keyv, roughly 127 million downloads a week, had their GitHub account taken and a credential-stealing worm pushed through the whole package family. Every one of those campaigns starts with a person, not a vulnerability. Reported by Aikido and Wiz, August 2026.

05

The policy

NIST SP 800-83

NIST SP 800-83 names four elements of malware prevention, policy, awareness, vulnerability mitigation, and threat mitigation. Policy comes first: in Stallings' words, it "provides a basis for implementing appropriate preventative countermeasures." This project owns that element.

"A security policy is a statement of what is, and what is not, allowed."

, Matt Bishop, Introduction to Computer Security (2005)
01

Policy

Documented rules that make prevention enforceable and assign accountability.

This project
02

Awareness

Teaching people to recognise the tricks before they arrive.

03

Vulnerability mitigation

Patching, least privilege, and hardening to shrink the attack surface.

04

Threat mitigation

Detection, containment, and response once something gets through.

01 · Purpose

People are the target

Social engineering attacks people, not machines. No antivirus stops a person being talked into trusting the wrong thing, only knowing the move does. This policy makes that knowledge the default.

02 · Scope

Everyone, every channel

All students, staff, and contractors. Any account or device. Every channel the attacks arrive through, phone, email, WhatsApp, in person, QR codes.

03 · The rules

Five that hold

Your password, OTP, and PIN are secret, nobody legitimate asks. Verify unexpected requests through a known channel. Urgency plus authority is a red flag, not a reason to rush. Don't run unknown files or plug in unknown drives. Turn on two-step verification.

04 · Reporting

Tell someone the same day

Report anything suspicious to ICT the same day. Good-faith reports are never punished, that line is what makes people report instead of hiding it.

05 · Roles

Shared responsibility

ICT owns and enforces it. HODs and deans promote it. Staff and students follow and report. One careless click can reach the whole network.

06 · Review

Kept current

Reviewed regularly, because the tricks change. Deepfakes were on no one's list two years ago. A policy that doesn't update is already out of date.

06

If it happens

First minutes

Falling for one of these isn't foolish, it's engineered to work on smart people in a hurry. What matters is the next few minutes.

Step 01

Cut it off

Stop replying. Send nothing more. If an account is involved, change the password from a device you trust.

Step 02

Call the real people

Your bank's official line, or FUTA ICT, the number you already know, never the one the scammer gave you.

Step 03

Warn your circle

If your account was used, tell your contacts it's compromised so the scam doesn't jump to them next.

Step 04

Report it, no shame

Reporting fast is how it gets stopped, for you and everyone after you. Good-faith reports are never punished.

This one's on all of us

This isn't only a school project. A scam can feel far off, something you read about in the news, right up until it isn't: someone close to you gets hit, an account gets taken, and because everyone is connected, you can become the next step in an attack without ever choosing to.

So guard the small things, because they are the whole game. Keep your passwords to yourself. Protect your privacy like it matters. Open links you trust, and when you don't trust one, open it somewhere that can't touch your machine. If you have to run code you didn't write, run it in a virtual machine.

None of it is hard. It's just paying attention. Let's make the web feel safe again, one careful click at a time.
